Etruscan Tombs in Italy: Blera, Tarquinia & the Via Clodia

Rock-cut necropolises and painted burial chambers older than Rome itself, still standing in the hills of northern Lazio.

Before Rome existed as more than a village, the Etruscans were building cities, roads, and elaborate rock-cut tombs across what's now northern Lazio and southern Tuscany. Much of what we know about Etruscan daily life — how they dressed, banqueted, danced, mourned — comes from what they carved and painted into their tombs. Unlike the Colosseum or the Forum, most of these sites see almost no international tourists. Here's where to actually see them.

Who were the Etruscans?

The Etruscans ("Rasna" or "Rasenna" in their own language — the word this site is named after) dominated central Italy from roughly the 8th to the 3rd century BCE, before being gradually absorbed into the expanding Roman Republic. They were skilled metalworkers, road-builders, and — based on what survives — deeply invested in how their dead were honored: entire "cities of the dead" (necropolises) carved directly into the soft volcanic tufo rock, often more elaborate than the houses of the living above them.

Blera's rock-cut necropolises

Blera, in Tuscia, sits directly on top of Etruscan foundations, and its surrounding countryside is dotted with rock-cut tomb facades cut straight into tufo cliff faces — some simple, some carved to resemble temple facades in miniature. Because Blera isn't a major tourist stop, you can walk among these largely alone, without ropes, ticket queues, or crowds — a genuinely different experience from a managed archaeological park.

Tarquinia: the UNESCO painted tombs

Tarquinia, about 40 minutes from Blera, holds the most important collection of painted Etruscan tombs in existence — a UNESCO World Heritage necropolis (Monterozzi) with frescoes over 2,500 years old still showing color: banquet scenes, dancers, animals, and the underworld imagery that gives us most of what we understand about Etruscan belief and daily life. The in-town Museo Archeologico Nazionale in Palazzo Vitelleschi displays artifacts recovered from the tombs, including the famous Winged Horses relief.

Visiting tips

  • Buy the combined ticket covering both the necropolis and the museum.
  • Several painted tombs are shown on rotation to limit light/humidity exposure — check which are open the day you visit.
  • Morning visits are cooler and quieter, especially in summer.

The Via Clodia: the road between them

The Via Clodia was an ancient Roman consular road built over earlier Etruscan routes, connecting Rome to the interior of Etruria. Long stretches survive near Blera as walkable tracks cut through tufo rock, passing directly by rock-cut tombs along the way — meaning you can walk an actual 2,000+ year-old road, tomb to tomb, rather than viewing them as isolated museum pieces.

Why these sites matter beyond the history

What strikes most visitors isn't just the age of these tombs — it's how undersold they are compared to Roman ruins of similar significance. There's no gift shop pressure, no timed-entry crowds, no roped-off distance from the stone. You can stand at the entrance to a 2,800-year-old tomb in near-total silence. That's rare almost anywhere else in Italy.
